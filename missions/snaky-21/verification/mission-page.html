Mission

Snaky in 21 Maker movesResearch Paper

CombinatoricsTheoretical Computer Science·Captain: wurtle

Motivation

In a Maker–Breaker achievement game, two players alternately claim cells of a board; Maker wins by owning every cell of some copy of a fixed shape, and Breaker only tries to prevent this. Polyomino achievement games on the infinite square grid, introduced by Frank Harary, ask which small polyominoes are winners (Maker can force a copy) and which are losers (Breaker can prevent it forever). The six-cell shape Snaky is the long-standing hard case: Maker was known to win with a one-stone handicap or in higher dimensions, but the ordinary planar game without handicap remained undecided.

Timeline

  • 2004. Sieben shows that Snaky is a winner in 41 dimensions and records earlier handicap-two strategies of Harary, Harborth and Seemann, as well as the non-existence of a pairing strategy for Breaker (Integers 4, 2004).
  • 2007. Halupczok and Schlage-Puchta prove that Snaky wins in three dimensions, loses on an 8×88\times88×8 board, and has planar handicap number at most one (Integers 7, 2007). Ito and Miyagawa give another handicap-one construction.
  • 2008. Sieben develops proof trees for weak achievement games and gives a finite proof sequence for the handicap-one win, describing the ordinary planar problem as undecided (Integers 8, 2008).
  • 2011. Csernenszky, Martin and Pluhár give a computer-free proof that Breaker has no pairing defense for Snaky (Integers 11, 2011).
  • 2023, 2025. Shaik, Mayer-Eichberger, van de Pol and Saffidine study QBF encodings of finite Maker–Breaker games, including Snaky on a 5×55\times55×5 board (arXiv:2301.07345); Boucher and Villemaire treat strong achievement games (doi:10.21428/594757db.6ae0c84a).

The source of this mission, an OpenAI preprint dated September 25, 2026, settles the ordinary planar game: Snaky is a winner, within 21 Maker moves.

Setting

The board is Z2\mathbb Z^2Z2, initially empty. Snaky is

S={(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0),(3,1),(4,1)}.S=\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0),(3,1),(4,1)\}.S={(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0),(3,1),(4,1)}.

An allowed copy is t+R(S)t+R(S)t+R(S) with t∈Z2t\in\mathbb Z^2t∈Z2 and RRR one of the eight 2×22\times22×2 signed permutation matrices (rotations by quarter turns and reflections; no scaling). Maker moves first; then Breaker and Maker alternate, each claiming one previously unclaimed cell. Maker wins when it owns all cells of an allowed copy.

A Maker policy is a rule that, given the current position, returns an unclaimed cell. In the fixed-endpoint formulation, the policy keeps claiming fresh cells even after a win, and one asks that after Maker's NNN-th claim its set of cells contain an allowed copy whenever Breaker's first N−1N-1N−1 replies were legal.

Significance

Theorem 1 decides the ordinary planar Snaky game, recorded as undecided by Sieben (2008), and gives an explicit bound of 21 Maker moves (a win by the 41st individual turn). It also gives a win on the finite 17×1717\times1717×17 board (Corollary 6), and removes the handicap from the earlier handicap-one strategies. The paper does not claim the bound 21 is optimal or address the strong game in which both players seek a copy.

The result is proved in an OpenAI preprint that has not been peer reviewed. Its correctness rests on a soundness lemma for a composition rule together with large finite certificates checked by standalone programs. A machine-checked proof would replace this combination of informal soundness argument and external computation by a single certified statement.

Difficulty

The board is infinite and Breaker may play anywhere, so no finite game-tree search applies directly: a proof must cover all Breaker replies, including those far from the action. Pairing strategies, the usual tool for proving shapes are losers, are known to fail for Snaky, but that does not give Maker a strategy. The certificate approach needs a composition rule whose soundness accounts for arbitrary previous ownership on both sides; the move count must be the number of actual Maker claims, including any replacement moves.

Formalization scope

  • Cells are ℤ × ℤ; snaky is the six-cell set above and HasSnaky M asks for r : Fin 8, t : Cell with snaky.image (placement r t) ⊆ M, where orient lists all eight signed permutations.
  • A Policy is a function of a move counter, the Maker set and the Breaker set; it must never return an owned cell. playState σ N β ∅ ∅ k is the position after kkk Maker claims and kkk Breaker replies.
  • LegalRepliesBeforeFinal σ N β ∅ ∅ constrains only the first N−1N-1N−1 Breaker replies; the theorems conclude a copy of SSS, exactly NNN Maker cells, and disjointness of the two players' sets throughout.
  • The certificate milestone stores the cards as an Array Entry and computes rowAt i (required set, envelope, height) by the composition recursion; it is a decidable statement about explicit finite data.

A trivializing reading is ruled out: the policy is required to be legal on all inputs, and Breaker's replies are arbitrary legal cells of the infinite board. A complete development needs a soundness proof for the composition rule (Lemma 3) and verified evaluation of the certificates; the policy, game and certificate infrastructure is shared between the 21- and 35-move statements. Contributions formalizing Lemma 3, Proposition 5 (the 21-move certificate identities) and Corollary 6 are welcome.

The theorem states that there is a policy σ for a Maker–Breaker-style placement game on the integer grid ℤ×ℤ, where a policy maps the number r of remaining rounds, the finite set M of cells already claimed by the maker, and the finite set B of cells already claimed by the blocker to a cell, such that σ always proposes a cell lying in neither M nor B, and such that for every blocker reply sequence β : ℕ → ℤ×ℤ that is legal before the final round, the following hold. Play starts from empty sets; at round k (counting from 0) with N = 21, the maker adds σ(21−k, M, B) to M and the blocker adds β(k) to B. Legality means that for every k with k+1 < 21, β(k) is not already in the maker's set, not the maker's cell chosen in that same round, and not already in the blocker's set. Under this legality assumption, the maker's set after 21 rounds contains a copy of the six-cell shape snaky = {(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0),(3,1),(4,1)}, placed by one of the eight rotations or reflections of the grid (as given by the orient map) followed by a translation. That final maker set has exactly 21 cells and is disjoint from the blocker's set after 20 rounds. Moreover, for every k < 21, the maker's and blocker's sets after k rounds are disjoint and the blocker's set has exactly k cells. The blocker's 21st reply is not constrained by the legality condition.
